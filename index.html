The common problem

Support for pupils with SEND often means a separate worksheet, a separate table, or a separate adult — and the pupil quietly gets less teaching from the teacher than anyone else in the room.

The adaptive response

The EEF’s evidence review found that what helps pupils with SEND most is high-quality teaching for everyone, adapted in the moment. Five approaches are especially well evidenced — the ‘Five-a-day’ — to be used daily and flexibly, as the starting point for all pupils.

Notice the barrierWhat, precisely, is getting in the way of this pupil learning this content?
Adapt the teachingWhich of the Five-a-day removes that barrier — for this pupil and, usually, for others too?
Keep the ambitionSame curriculum, same goal, same standard — the route changes, not the destination.

Think of one pupil with SEND you teach or support. In one line: what do they find hardest in your lessons — not their diagnosis, the actual moment?

Vignette · Science, age 13 · AmaraSpeech, language and communication needs
1 · NOTICE

Mr Adeyemi gives the practical instructions in one flowing explanation. Amara nods, then sits still while others start. By the time she asks a friend, the class is on step three. She is marked “off task”.

2 · ADAPT

Next lesson he chunks the method into four numbered steps on the board, models step one under the visualiser, and asks everyone to show the next step on mini-whiteboards before anyone touches equipment. Pairs do steps two and three together; step four is independent.

3 · KEEP

Same practical, same equipment, same conclusion expected in the write-up. Amara completes all four steps. So do three pupils who had been copying quietly for weeks.

What the evidence says
  • Teacher-led: explain, model, check for understanding, guide, then independent practice.EEF SEND guidance
  • Small steps, many questions, worked models, a high success rate before independence.Rosenshine 2012
  • Not lecturing — the checks decide who you guide next.
Through both lenses
TeacherUse clear, succinct language. Chunk content into small steps. Model the task before expecting independence.
Teaching assistantRe-explain with the same words the teacher used. Dual-code on a mini-whiteboard. Check understanding by asking the pupil to say the next step back.
Try this tomorrowChunk the next method into four numbered steps on the board. Mini-whiteboards: all 32 show step one before anyone starts.
Say this“Show me step one on your board. Not sure? Hold it up anyway — that tells me who I’m coming to.”
Track thisAt the check, how many boards were wrong, and on which step? That step is tomorrow’s re-teach.
If it continuesSame three or four pupils stall at the same step for two weeks: agree a five-minute pre-teach of the key words with the TA before the lesson.
Check: put the sequence in order

Vignette · Maths, age 10 · DanielAttention and working-memory difficulties
1 · NOTICE

Long division. Daniel can do each step when asked, but mid-problem he loses where he is, starts again, and gives up after the second restart. The worksheet has twelve problems crammed on one page.

2 · ADAPT

Ms Ferreira opens with three retrieval questions on times tables so the facts are ready. She thinks aloud through a worked example — “What do I do first? How will I know I’m right?” — and gives the class a plan–do–check strip. New sheets: three problems per page, generous space.

3 · KEEP

Same problems, same method. Daniel finishes eight of twelve and, for the first time, tells the TA which step he got wrong before she does.

What the evidence says
  • Working memory is small: cut clutter, split attention and too many steps at once.Sweller 1988
  • Teaching pupils to plan, monitor and evaluate adds about seven months’ progress.EEF 2018
  • Pupils with SEND are often the last to be taught how to learn — and gain most.
Through both lenses
TeacherStart with recall of prior content. Model the selection of a strategy out loud. Give a checklist pupils can use to monitor themselves.
Teaching assistantAsk metacognitive questions rather than giving the next step: “What did you do last time?” “Which step are you on?” “How will you check?”
Try this tomorrowCut the worksheet to three problems per page with working space. Open with three retrieval questions so the facts are ready.
Say this“Before you start: what is your first step? How will you know you are right?”
Track thisTally how many pupils restart a problem from the top. When the tally falls, the load is right.
If it continuesA pupil still loses their place after chunking: add a plan–do–check strip on the desk, shorten the steps again, review in two weeks.
Check: plan, monitor or evaluate?

Vignette · Writing, age 9 · LeahDyslexia
1 · NOTICE

A persuasive letter. Leah talks through brilliant arguments in the paired discussion, then writes two lines in twenty minutes. Every sentence stalls at a spelling. She has a differentiated sheet: “write three sentences”.

2 · ADAPT

Mr Bakshi gives the whole class a partially completed letter and a bank of persuasive openers and topic words; anyone can use them. Week two: openers only. Week three: a checklist of features, no frame. Leah keeps the word bank a week longer than most — by choice, not by table.

3 · KEEP

Same genre, same success criteria, same audience. Leah’s week-three letter has four paragraphs and an argument the class votes most convincing.

What the evidence says
  • Support lets a learner do today what they cannot do alone — so that tomorrow they can.Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976
  • Visual, verbal or written; reduced over the lesson, the week or the term.EEF
  • Offered to the whole class with opt-in, so nobody is singled out.

A scaffold that never comes down is not a scaffold. It is a ceiling.

Through both lenses
TeacherPlan the fade before you plan the frame. Offer scaffolds at whole-class level so pupils opt in without being singled out.
Teaching assistantGive scaffolds to a pair or small group rather than one pupil. Notice when a pupil no longer looks at the frame — and quietly remove it.
Try this tomorrowGive the word bank or sentence frame to all 30; let pupils opt in. Write the fade date on the plan before you print the frame.
Say this“Use the frame if you need it today. Next week we try without the openers.”
Track thisEach week, note who still looks at the frame. When they stop looking — remove it.
If it continuesStill needs the frame after three weeks: the barrier may be different (vocabulary? transcription?). Notice again before adding another scaffold.
Check: fading or fixed?

Vignette · English, age 14 · JaydenSocial, emotional and mental health — anxiety
1 · NOTICE

Jayden has sat on the “support table” since September. He knows what the table means; so does everyone. He avoids the work before it is set, asks to leave, and a mistake in front of the group ends his lesson.

2 · ADAPT

Ms Nakamura drops the fixed table. After Monday’s exit ticket she forms three temporary groups by the skill each pupil missed. Jayden joins the inference group for ten minutes on Tuesday, then re-joins the class. On Thursday he leads a vocabulary pair — it was the one thing he got right.

3 · KEEP

Same whole-class text, same essay question. Jayden stays in the room all week. Groups dissolve as the need dissolves.

What the evidence says
  • Fixed setting and streaming: little overall benefit; tends to disadvantage lower-attaining pupils.EEF Toolkit
  • Temporary within-class grouping by current need: a modest positive effect.EEF Toolkit
  • Structured peer tutoring is among the more effective low-cost approaches.EEF Toolkit

The word that matters is temporary: a group formed for a difficulty with today’s content, dissolved when the difficulty is resolved.

Through both lenses
TeacherGroup on current difficulty with the content, not on label or prior attainment. Rotate who tutors whom so every pupil is sometimes the expert.
Teaching assistantWork with several different pupils across the week. If the same three pupils are always yours, the group has become fixed.
Try this tomorrowAfter Monday’s exit ticket, form three ten-minute groups by the skill missed. Dissolve them on Friday.
Say this“This group is for inference today. Tomorrow it will be different people.”
Track thisJot who was in which group each week. If the same names recur, the group has become fixed.
If it continuesOne pupil misses the same skill three weeks running: that is a barrier to assess and plan for — not a reason to re-group.
Check: what made Ms Nakamura’s grouping flexible?

Vignette · History, age 11 · SofiaSpeech, language and literacy difficulties
1 · NOTICE

Sofia’s oral answers about the causes of the fire are the richest in the class. Her written explanation is three fragments. The gap between what she knows and what she can show is widening — and her grades follow the writing.

2 · ADAPT

Mrs Haddad puts a pupil’s draft paragraph under the visualiser and the class reworks it together — what makes an explanation, not just a description. Sofia then dictates her paragraph with speech-to-text on a tablet and edits the text against the class checklist. Her finished paragraph goes under the visualiser next.

3 · KEEP

Same question, same criteria, same word count. The technology removed the transcription barrier; the thinking was always Sofia’s.

What the evidence says
  • Technology helps when it has a clear teaching purpose: modelling, practice, feedback.EEF 2019
  • Two adaptive uses: teacher modelling (visualiser) and pupil recording (speech-to-text).
  • Changing the medium alone does not remove the barrier.

Ask of any tool: which barrier does this remove, and does the pupil still do the thinking?

Through both lenses
TeacherUse a visualiser to model and to jointly rework an incorrect example. Make speech-to-text and audio recording ordinary options for recording learning.
Teaching assistantUse technology inside interventions, and support pupils to set up and edit their own speech-to-text — the goal is that they run it without you.
Try this tomorrowPut one pupil’s draft under the visualiser and improve it with the class. Offer speech-to-text to anyone for a first draft.
Say this“Say your sentence into the tablet, read it back, then fix it against the checklist.”
Track thisWord count and number of edits: is the pupil doing the thinking and the editing?
If it continuesTranscription still blocks the ideas: agree one consistent tool with the SENCO so it travels between lessons.
Check: what kind of use is this?

What the evidence says
  • Pupils who received the most TA support made less progress than similar pupils who received less.DISS · Blatchford 2009
  • Not because of the TAs — because TAs replaced the teacher for the pupils who most needed the teacher.
  • TAs add value when they supplement teaching rather than replace it, and when they build independence.EEF · Making Best Use of TAs
The TA scaffolding ladder

Least help first. Step down only when the pupil is stuck; step back up as soon as they move.

1Self-scaffolding — wait. Let the pupil try.
2Prompting — “What could you try first?”
3Clueing — a hint towards the strategy, not the answer.
4Modelling — show it, then hand it back.
5Correcting — the last resort, not the first.
▲ Least help · start hereMost help ▼
The teacher plans the adaptation and owns the pupil’s progress. The TA delivers it moment by moment — and reports back what the pupil could do alone. Neither role is “the SEND person”.
